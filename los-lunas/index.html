Keeping Your Los Lunas Water Heater Running Longer

A little routine care makes all the difference between a water heater that runs for years and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a yearly flush, an anode rod check every three to five years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a temperature setting around 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The serial number on the label usually encodes the manufacture date, and its age tells you a lot. Most tank heaters make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units about 20. If your heater is past that range and also shows rusty water, popping sounds, or dampness under the tank, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble can often be fixed with a flush, yet that same buildup, ignored through a few more years, can overheat the bottom of the tank and shorten the life of the tank, turning a simple visit into a replacement. Save our number on your phone now so help is one call away the moment something sounds or looks wrong. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Most water heater problems we see in Los Lunas come back to the water itself. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Then there's age. When the sacrificial anode rod is gone, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the steel starts to rust from the inside, and a drip from the bottom seam often shows up next. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Well water may bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a different rod can fix. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a properly routed T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak would hit finished floors, and seismic straps in areas that require them. All of these can be fixed, and most cost far less when caught early.
